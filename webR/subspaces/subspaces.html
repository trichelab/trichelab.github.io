Subspaces, embeddings, and all that

“Siri, make me a UMAP”

Before making spin art plots of tens (or perhaps hundreds) of thousands of dollars’ worth of data, one might consider what precisely is happening under the hood. Usually, this is prompted by the similar events to any trip under a hood – “the machinery isn’t doing what I want it to!” Fortunately, the tools to diagnose typical UMAP disasters are cheaper than the OBD reader for a car.

How the spin art works (or doesn’t)

Nonlinear dimension reduction schemes like tSNE and UMAP typically operate in reduced dimensions. Given a number of linear dimensions, tSNE and UMAP attempt to maximize the separation of minimally similar data points and vice versa. The raw data is usually compressed by taking linear combinations of features, since the usefulness of the results depends upon how similarity is measured. (Trivia: the X and Y coordinates of a UMAP embedding change when you add a Z…)

For example, with a single-cell RNAseq dataset, the features are transcripts, the typical compression is a principal components analysis (PCA) with 30 or so dimensions, and often the defaults involve throwing away 80% to 90% of the data by relying only on highly variable features (HVG, “highly variable genes”).

An old trick from noisy microarray expression experiments is to mask features. An older trick from statistics is to mask off entire combinations of features. Suppose Factor 10 = 0.5 * GENE1 + 2.6 * GENE2 - 1.3 * GENE345. Now suppose that Factor 10 is almost entirely dependent upon technical artifacts that we don’t care about and don’t want in our colorful main figure. We can simply drop it. The remaining dimensions are referred to as a subspace of the original data.

A concrete example: classifying leukemia by cell of origin

Instead of a typical single-cell dataset, we are going to train a model on DNA methylation from leukemia patients and sorted healthy progenitor cells. Modern evidence-based understanding of hematopoiesis (blood production) has settled around a scheme where hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) provide a lifetime of blood supply, mostly by delegating the work to shorter-lived multipotent progenitors (MPPs). These in turn generate three major classes of primed progenitors: megakaryocyte-erythrocyte progenitors (MEPs), which make platelets and red blood cells; granulocyte-monocyte progenitors (GMPs), which make what you would expect from the name; and lymphoid-primed multipotent progenitors, or LMPPs, which seem to be where B and T cells come from (but also some other cell types, it’s all a bit mysterious). A diagram from Grant Challen’s Exp Hem paper is below; it will may clarify or further murky-ify the topic.

If these cells refuse to differentiate and instead proliferate, that’s leukemia. Certain mutations occur repeatedly in leukemia, and patients are often assigned to specific treatment based on the mutations observed in their disease. However, the cell from which their leukemia arose may better predict their responses. Historically, that has been harder to model, but the nice thing about ‘epigenomic’ assays is that they are great at recovering this. The really nice thing about these assays is that they are cheap and fast, too.

Modeling normal and abnormal progenitors

Let’s pull in the raw data from 120 leukemia patients and 5 healthy donors. The healthy donors provided bone marrow which was then sorted to yield MEP, GMP, and LMPP fractions (separated on the basis of cell surface antigens). The data is summarized as DNA methylation fractions across genomic coordinates, and the column names indicate who is who and what is what.

Code 
aml <- read.csv(url("https://trichelab.github.io/data/aml.csv"), row=1)
meta <- data.frame(do.call(rbind, strsplit(colnames(aml), "_")))
names(meta) <- c("ID", "status", "lineage")
with(meta, table(lineage, status))
       status
lineage AML Control
   GMP   93       5
   LMPP  12       5
   MEP   15       5

We can immediately observe two things: * The control set (MEP, GMP, LMPP) is balanced. * The interesting set (leukemia patients) is severely imbalanced.

Let’s see how this impacts our downstream adventures.

Fitting the data

Let’s skip a few steps (more on this later) and fit an appropriate-dimensional nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF) model of the data. (Why NMF? Because you can’t have negative amounts of DNA methylation, among other reasons.)
